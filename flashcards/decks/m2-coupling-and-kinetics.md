# Deck: M2 — Coupling, ATP & kinetics
tags: m2 thermodynamics kinetics foundations

Q: How does a cell make an uphill reaction (ΔG > 0) happen?
A: Reaction coupling. Free energy adds, so link it to a downhill reaction (e.g. ATP hydrolysis, −30 kJ/mol) sharing a common intermediate; if the summed ΔG < 0 it goes.

---

Q: Coupling requires what, mechanically — not just "energy released nearby"?
A: The two reactions must share a chemical intermediate on the same enzyme (typically a phosphate transferred from ATP onto the substrate — phosphorylation).

---

Q: Why does ATP hydrolysis release free energy? (3 reasons from M1)
A: (1) Electrostatic strain — ~4 crammed negative charges repel; splitting relieves it. (2) Entropy — pieces separate. (3) Resonance + solvation stabilise the released Pᵢ. Not a mystical "high-energy bond."

---

Q: Why is the cell's actual ΔG of ATP hydrolysis (~−50) more negative than ΔG° (~−30)?
A: The cell holds a high ATP/ADP ratio (products ADP, Pᵢ kept scarce). Via ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q, small Q → more negative ΔG. A battery held charged.

---

Q: Mechanically, how do plentiful products slow a reaction?
A: They don't slow the forward reaction — they speed the REVERSE one (rate_rev = k_r[products]), shrinking net rate. Equilibrium = forward rate equals reverse rate.

---

Q: What is the activation energy (Eₐ), and what sets a reaction's rate?
A: Eₐ is the height of the transition-state peak above the reactants — the toll every reaction pays, even downhill ones. Rate ∝ e^(−Eₐ/RT), so small Eₐ changes cause huge rate changes.

---

Q: Why can a reaction with large negative ΔG still be effectively frozen (e.g. a sugar cube)?
A: ΔG (well depth) sets how favourable, not how fast. If Eₐ (the barrier) is high, the Boltzmann fraction of molecules that can cross is negligible at room temperature, so it doesn't proceed.

---

Q: Does a catalyst change how FAR a reaction goes, how FAST, or both? Why?
A: Only how fast. It lowers the peak (Eₐ) via an alternate route, speeding both directions equally, but leaves ΔG / well depths untouched → equilibrium position and K are unchanged. Same destination, sooner.

---

Q: How does an enzyme actually lower the activation barrier?
A: Its folded active site binds and stabilises the transition state more tightly than the reactant — that is what lowers the peak. Hence the importance of folding/charge/H-bonds (M1). Enzymes: 10⁶–10¹⁷× faster, highly specific.

---

Q: Climate: contrast RuBisCO and carbonic anhydrase kinetically.
A: RuBisCO (carbon fixation) is slow (~a few/s) AND unspecific (also fixes O₂ → photorespiration) — a prime engineering target. Carbonic anhydrase is near the diffusion limit (about as fast as physically possible).
