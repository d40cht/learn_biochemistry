# Deck: M1 — Chemical foundations
tags: m1 foundations chemistry

Q: What is electronegativity?
A: An atom's "greed" for the shared electrons in a covalent bond. O and N are greedy; C and H are mild.

---

Q: Covalent vs ionic bond — what's the difference in terms of electrons?
A: Covalent = electrons shared between nuclei. Ionic = electrons transferred outright, giving two fully charged ions held by Coulomb attraction. Same spectrum, set by electronegativity difference.

---

Q: What makes a covalent bond polar vs nonpolar?
A: Unequal electronegativity → electrons lean to the greedy atom → partial charges (δ−/δ+) → polar. Equal greed (C–C, C–H) → nonpolar.

---

Q: What is a hydrogen bond, and which atoms are involved?
A: Attraction between a δ+ hydrogen (bonded to O, N, or F) and a lone pair on a nearby greedy atom (O/N). Weak individually (~1/20 of a covalent bond), directional, decisive in bulk.

---

Q: Why does NaCl dissolve in water? (Name the interaction.)
A: Ion–dipole interactions: water's δ− oxygens swarm Na⁺, its δ+ hydrogens swarm Cl⁻, pulling the ions apart. NOT hydrogen bonds (NaCl has no H).

---

Q: What actually drives the hydrophobic effect?
A: Water's entropy. Water around a nonpolar surface must order into a cage (low entropy, costly); clustering the nonpolar stuff together minimises exposed surface and frees the water. The grease isn't repelled — the water is freed.

---

Q: What is the dominant driving force of protein folding, and what does it do?
A: The hydrophobic effect — it buries greasy side chains in a core and exposes polar/charged ones, collapsing the chain into a specific shape. Backbone H-bonds, van der Waals packing, salt bridges, and disulfides fine-tune the winner.

---

Q: Why do membrane proteins fold "inside-out"?
A: The lipid bilayer is nonpolar, the opposite of water. So segments in the membrane put greasy residues facing OUT (toward lipid). Proof that the environment, not the residues, dictates the fold.

---

Q: Define pH.
A: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Pure water [H⁺]=10⁻⁷ → pH 7 (neutral); lower = acidic, higher = basic. Log scale: one unit = 10× the H⁺.

---

Q: Brønsted–Lowry: acid vs base. And the Lewis generalisation?
A: Acid = proton donor, base = proton acceptor. Lewis (deeper): acid = electron-pair acceptor, base = electron-pair donor; a proton is the simplest electron-pair acceptor.

---

Q: In electron terms, what happens when an acid dissociates?
A: It releases a proton while KEEPING the bonding electrons → becomes A⁻ (negative). The bare proton lands on a water lone pair → H₃O⁺. The H–A bond is polar covalent; more polar = stronger acid.

---

Q: What makes a molecule a strong(er) acid?
A: Stability of its conjugate base A⁻: an electronegative atom holding the charge, resonance spreading it (e.g. carboxylate –COO⁻ over two oxygens), and inductive electron-withdrawal.

---

Q: What is a buffer and why does it resist pH change?
A: A mix of a weak acid (HA) and its conjugate base (A⁻), both stocked in large amounts. Add H⁺ → A⁻ mops it up (→HA); add OH⁻ → HA donates H⁺. Capacity is finite; strongest near pKₐ.

---

Q: Why doesn't a buffer's HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ just run to completion, leaving no buffering capacity?
A: Equilibrium is a dynamic, populated mixture (minimum of free energy G=H−TS), not exhaustion. A weak acid only partially dissociates, so big reservoirs of BOTH HA and A⁻ coexist. (See M2.)

---

Q: What is pKₐ?
A: The pH at which an acid is exactly half-dissociated ([A⁻]=[HA]). A buffer works best near its pKₐ. From pH = pKₐ + log([A⁻]/[HA]).

---

Q: Which reaction is both the bicarbonate blood buffer and ocean acidification?
A: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. More atmospheric CO₂ → more H⁺ → lower ocean pH. Sped up by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (a carbon-capture target).

---

Q: At physiological pH, what charge do –COOH (Asp/Glu) and –NH₂ (Lys) side chains carry, and why is histidine special?
A: –COOH → –COO⁻ (negative, donated its proton); –NH₂ → –NH₃⁺ (positive, grabbed a proton). Histidine's pKₐ ≈ 6, near physiological pH, so it flips charge easily → key proton shuttle in enzyme active sites.
