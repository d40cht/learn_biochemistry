# Problem: How much ATP can one NADH pay for? (M10)

**Concept exercised:** ΔG = −nFΔE linking redox potential to free energy, and to ATP yield.

## Statement
In the electron transport chain, electrons pass from NADH (E°′ = −0.32 V) to O₂
(E°′ = +0.82 V), transferring n = 2 electrons. (a) Compute ΔG°′ for this transfer.
(b) If making one ATP costs about 50 kJ/mol (the cellular value), how many ATP could that
free energy pay for in principle? (c) Cells actually make ~2.5 ATP per NADH — comment.

## Approach
ΔE°′ = E°′(acceptor) − E°′(donor); ΔG°′ = −nF·ΔE°′ with F = 96.5 kJ·mol⁻¹·V⁻¹.
Then divide the available free energy by the per-ATP cost.

## Solution
(a) ΔE°′ = 0.82 − (−0.32) = **1.14 V**.
ΔG°′ = −nFΔE°′ = −(2)(96.5)(1.14) ≈ **−220 kJ/mol**. (Large and negative → strongly
spontaneous.)

(b) 220 / 50 ≈ **4.4 ATP** worth of free energy in principle.

(c) Cells harvest ~2.5 ATP per NADH, so efficiency ≈ 2.5 / 4.4 ≈ **~57%**. The rest is
"lost" as heat — but not wasted: that dissipation is what keeps the whole chain running
forward (far from equilibrium), and warm-blooded animals use some of it deliberately.

## Sanity check
A ~1 V drop over 2 electrons giving ~220 kJ/mol is the right ballpark (≈ 2.3 eV per
electron-pair). ~2.5 ATP/NADH is the standard textbook stoichiometry — consistent.

## Takeaway
Redox potential differences are just free energy in volts. The big NADH→O₂ drop is the
power source of aerobic life: catabolism loads electrons onto NADH, and the chain cashes
that ~220 kJ/mol in as ATP (M12). The same ΔG = −nFΔE accounting governs photosynthesis's
light reactions, which run electrons "uphill" using photon energy to make NADPH (M13).
